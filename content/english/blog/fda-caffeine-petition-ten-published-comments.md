On October 19, 2026, the FDA posted the tenth public comment on Food Foundations’ petition about caffeine labeling for dietary supplements. It came from [The Weston Firm](https://www.regulations.gov/comment/FDA-2026-P-8648-0040). Two comments posted on October 7 had looked at the same issue from a reader’s perspective. All three start with a question someone might ask when picking up a supplement. How much caffeine would I get if I followed the directions on this package?

## One scoop, two scoops or the whole container

[The Weston Firm](https://www.regulations.gov/comment/FDA-2026-P-8648-0040) supported adding up caffeine from every source in a supplement and showing the total in milligrams. An ingredient list might name several sources, including botanicals, without telling the reader how much caffeine they contain altogether. Even a label that gives the weight of a blend leaves a question unanswered. How much of that weight is caffeine?

The firm pointed to another part of the package that can change what a reader needs to know. Suppose the directions say to use one or two scoops. Someone using two scoops still has to do the math if the amount on the label is for one. The firm asked the FDA to consider requiring the caffeine amount for the largest recommended single dose. It also supported showing the total for a container someone would reasonably finish in one sitting, with records to back up the figures on the label.

## How readers make sense of a number

[Norman Wong](https://www.regulations.gov/comment/FDA-2026-P-8648-0038) suggested giving people something familiar to compare the number with, such as cups of coffee. He also suggested putting the amount in the context of a day’s caffeine intake. His comment raised a question about label design. Once a reader has found the amount in milligrams, what would help them make sense of it?

[Michael Paasche-Orlow](https://www.regulations.gov/comment/FDA-2026-P-8648-0039), a primary care physician who studies health literacy, supported clear, concise disclosure. He also pointed out that some people would still need help using that information. A label can put the number where someone can find it and still leave them with a question about what it means for them.

## What changed in October

Our [August update](https://www.foodfoundationsproject.org/blog/fda-caffeine-petition-gains-early-momentum-7-comments-and-regulatory-coverage-in-three-weeks/) covered the first seven public comments. The FDA received Wong’s and Paasche-Orlow’s comments on September 30 and posted both on October 7. It received the law firm’s comment on October 9 and posted it on October 19, bringing the published count to ten.

Families and partners can read the original request, the supporting material and each public comment in [docket FDA-2026-P-8648](https://www.regulations.gov/docket/FDA-2026-P-8648). The petition remains open. The October additions record what three commenters want the FDA to consider as it reviews the request.
